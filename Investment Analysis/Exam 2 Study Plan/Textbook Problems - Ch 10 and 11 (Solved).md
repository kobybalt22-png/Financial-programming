### #4: Invoice price
A semiannual bond, coupon 6%, ask price 117% of \$1,000 par, last coupon paid one month ago.
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Flat price = 1.17 × 1,000 = \$1,170
Accrued interest = (60/2) × (1 month / 6 months) = 30 × 1/6 = \$5
**Invoice price = \$1,175**
</details>

### #6: Why do bond prices fall when rates rise? Don't investors like high rates?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

A bond's coupons are fixed. When market rates rise, the PV of those fixed payments falls, because each one is discounted at a higher rate. Equivalently, a new buyer won't pay full price for a low coupon when new bonds pay more, so the price drops until its yield matches the market. Investors *do* like high rates **for money they invest from now on**, but bonds they already own lose value.
</details>

### #20: Treasury quote with 32nds
7% semiannual bond, coupons Jan 15 & Jul 15. Ask price on **Jan 30** = **100:02**. Coupon period = 182 days.
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**"100:02" means 100 and 2/32 percent of par**: 100.0625% × 1,000 = \$1,000.625
Days since last coupon: Jan 15 → Jan 30 = 15
Accrued interest = 35 × 15/182 = \$2.885
**Invoice price ≈ \$1,003.51**
</details>

### #27: Another invoice price (same format as #20)

**Invoice price ≈ \$1,036.25**

### #10: Holding-period return
8-year bond with a 6% current yield and a 6% annual coupon. One year later, YTM rises to 7%.
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

CY = coupon rate, so the bond is at **par** (\$1,000) today.
One year later, 7 years left: N = 7, I/Y = 7, PMT = 60, FV = 1,000, so the price = **\$946.11**
HPR = (60 + 946.11 − 1,000) / 1,000 = **0.61%**
</details>

### #12: Which has the higher effective annual rate?
(a) A 3-month T-bill, \$100,000 face, price \$97,645. (b) A par coupon bond, 10% paid semiannually.
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

(a) 3-month return = 100,000/97,645 − 1 = 2.412%. EAR = 1.02412⁴ − 1 = **10.00%**
(b) EAR = 1.05² − 1 = **10.25%**, so **the coupon bond is higher**.
</details>

### #14: Total return over 6 months
10% coupon (semiannual), market rate 4% per half-year, 3 years to maturity. (a) Price today and in 6 months? (b) Total return?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

(a) Today: N = 6, I/Y = 4, PMT = 50, FV = 1,000, so **\$1,052.42**
In 6 months: N = 5, so **\$1,044.52**
(b) Return = (50 + 1,044.52 − 1,052.42) / 1,052.42 = **4.0%** per 6 months. It equals the market rate, because the yield didn't change.
</details>

### #15: BEY and EAY
20-year, 8% semiannual bond. Find the BEY and EAY at prices of (a) \$950, (b) \$1,000, (c) \$1,050.
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

N = 40, PMT = 40, FV = 1,000

| Price | I/Y (semi) | BEY (× 2) | EAY ((1+r)² − 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| \$950 | 4.263% | **8.53%** | **8.71%** |
| \$1,000 | 4.000% | **8.00%** | **8.16%** |

</details>

### #21 and #22: Current yield vs. YTM
A bond has CY = 9% and YTM = 10%. (#21) Above or below par? (#22) Is its coupon rate more or less than 9%?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

#21: CY < YTM, so it's a **discount bond**, selling **below par**.
#22: For a discount bond, coupon < CY < YTM, so the coupon rate is **less than 9%**.
</details>

### #7: Callable at 105 vs. callable at 110
Two bonds are identical except that one is callable at 105 and the other at 110. Which should have the higher YTM?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**The bond callable at 105.** A lower call price makes calling cheaper for the issuer, which is worse for the investor. Investors demand compensation, so it sells at a lower price, which means a higher YTM.
</details>

### #26: Realized compound yield
2-year bond, \$100 annual coupon, price \$1,000. YTM? RCY if next year's 1-year rate is (a) 8%, (b) 10%, (c) 12%?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Price = par, so **YTM = 10%**.
Terminal wealth = 1,100 (final coupon + face) + 100 × (1 + r) (first coupon, reinvested for 1 year)
RCY = (TW / 1,000)^(1/2) − 1

| Reinvest rate | Terminal wealth | RCY |
|---|---|---|
| 8% | \$1,208 | **9.91%** |
| 10% | \$1,210 | **10.00%** |
| 12% | \$1,212 | **10.09%** |

This matches the slide rule: reinvest < YTM gives RCY < YTM.
</details>

### #30 (slide #32): Yield to call
30-year, **8%** semiannual bond (your book; the slide's version says 6%), callable in 5 years at \$1,100. It currently sells at a YTM of 7% (3.5% per half-year). (a) YTC? (b) YTC if the call price is \$1,050? (c) YTC if callable at \$1,100 in **2** years?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1, price first:** N = 60, I/Y = 3.5, PMT = 40, FV = 1,000, so **P = \$1,124.72**
(a) N = 10, PV = −1,124.72, PMT = 40, FV = 1,100: I/Y = 3.37%, so **YTC = 6.74%**
(b) Same, but FV = 1,050: I/Y = 2.98%, so **YTC = 5.95%**
(c) N = 4, FV = 1,100: I/Y = 3.03%, so **YTC = 6.06%**
*If your version uses a 6% coupon, do the same steps with PMT = 30.*
</details>

### #8: Price over time
A 10% coupon bond has YTM 8%. If the YTM stays the same, will the price be higher, lower, or unchanged in a year?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Lower.** Coupon > YTM makes it a premium bond. With an unchanged yield, a premium bond's price drifts **down toward par** as maturity approaches (pull to par).
</details>

# PART B: Deck 3, Term Structure (Ch. 10, p. 325–326 / PDF 353–354)

### #34: True/false/uncertain
Under the expectations hypothesis, an upward-sloping yield curve means the market expects short-term rates to rise.
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**True.** Under the expectations hypothesis, forward rate = expected future short rate. An upward slope means forward rates are above today's short rate, so rates are expected to rise.
</details>

### #35: An upward-sloping curve: do investors expect rates to rise?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Not necessarily.** Under liquidity preference theory, long rates include a liquidity premium. The curve can slope up even if short rates are expected to stay flat.
</details>

### #37: True/false/uncertain
Under liquidity preference theory, if inflation is expected to fall, long-term rates will be higher than short-term rates.
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Uncertain.** Falling inflation means short rates are expected to fall, which pushes the curve **down**. The liquidity premium pushes it **up**. The net effect depends on which force is bigger.
</details>

### #38 (slide #40): Forward rates and next year's curve
Zero yields: 1-yr 10%, 2-yr 11%, 3-yr 12%. (a) Implied 1-year forward rates? (b) Under the pure expectations hypothesis, what will the yield curve be next year? (c) Expected 1-year return on a 2-year zero and on a 3-year zero?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

(a) f₂ = 1.11²/1.10 − 1 = **12.01%**. f₃ = 1.12³/1.11² − 1 = **14.03%**
(b) Next year's 1-year rate = f₂ = **12.01%**. Next year's 2-year rate = [(1.1201)(1.1403)]^(1/2) − 1 = **13.02%**
(c) 2-year zero: price now = 1,000/1.11² = \$811.62. Next year (a 1-year zero) = 1,000/1.1201 = \$892.78. Return = **10.0%**
3-year zero: now = 1,000/1.12³ = \$711.78. Next year (a 2-year zero) = 1,000/1.1302² = \$782.96. Return = **10.0%**
**Point:** under the expectations hypothesis, every maturity earns the same expected return over the year, which is today's 1-year rate of 10%.
</details>

### #39 (slide #41): Forward rate and the two theories
1-yr zero = 8%, 2-yr zero = 9%. (a) Forward rate for year 2? (b) Expected short rate next year under the expectations hypothesis? (c) Under liquidity preference, is it higher or lower than (b)?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

(a) f₂ = 1.09²/1.08 − 1 = **10.01%**
(b) **10.01%** (forward = expected rate)
(c) **Lower.** Forward = expected rate + liquidity premium, so expected rate = forward − premium.
</details>

### #41 (slide #43): Expected rate three years from now
\$1,000 par zeros: 1-yr 5%, 2-yr 6%, 3-yr 6.5%, 4-yr 7%. Under the expectations hypothesis, what is the expected 1-year rate **three years from now** (that is, during year 4)?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

f₄ = 1.07⁴ / 1.065³ − 1 = 1.31080 / 1.20795 − 1 = **8.51%**
Watch the wording: "three years from now" means the rate from the end of year 3 to the end of year 4, which is **f₄**.
</details>

# PART C: Deck 2, Interest Rate Risk (Ch. 11, p. 352–353 / PDF 380–381)

### #4: Backing out duration from a price change
Price \$1,050, YTM 6%. If the yield rises 25 bps, the price falls to \$1,025. What's the duration?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

%ΔP = −25/1,050 = −2.381%
D_Mod = −(%ΔP)/Δy = 0.02381 / 0.0025 = **9.52 years**
Macaulay (annual coupons assumed) = 9.52 × 1.06 = **10.1 years**
</details>

### #8: Duration table
6% annual coupon, 3 years to maturity. Find the duration at YTM = 6% and at YTM = 10%.
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**YTM 6% (price \$1,000):**

| t | CF | PV | Weight | t × w |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60 | 56.60 | 0.0566 | 0.0566 |
| 2 | 60 | 53.40 | 0.0534 | 0.1068 |
| 3 | 1,060 | 890.00 | 0.8900 | 2.6700 |

**D = 2.833 years**

**YTM 10% (price \$900.53):** weights 0.0606, 0.0551, 0.8844, so **D = 2.824 years**

Higher YTM gives slightly **lower** duration, because the distant cash flows lose weight.
</details>

### #9: % price change from duration
9-year bond, yield 10%, (Macaulay) duration 7.194. The yield changes 50 bps. % price change?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

D_Mod = 7.194 / 1.10 = 6.54
%ΔP ≈ −6.54 × 0.005 = **−3.27%** (or +3.27% if the yield falls)
</details>

### #12: Rates fall 10 bps
Asset duration = 5 years, rates = 8%, rates fall 10 bps. Expected % price increase?
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

D_Mod = 5 / 1.08 = 4.63
%ΔP ≈ 4.63 × 0.001 = **+0.463%**
</details>

### #14: Which bond gains more if rates fall?
You expect rates to fall from about 8%. Pick the bond in each pair with the bigger capital gain.
(a) Baa 8% 20-yr vs. **Aaa** 8% 20-yr. (b) 4% coupon 20-yr callable at 105 vs. 8% coupon 20-yr callable at 105. (c) 6% coupon 20-yr T-bond vs. 9% coupon 20-yr T-bond (both YTM 8%).
<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

(a) **The Aaa bond.** Its YTM is lower, so it has a higher duration. It's also a pure bet on rates, with no credit-spread risk.
(b) **The 4% coupon bond.** It has a higher duration (lower coupon). It's also a deep-discount bond, so it's unlikely to be called, while the 8% bond's gains would be capped by the call price.
(c) **The 6% coupon bond.** A lower coupon means a higher duration.
</details>
